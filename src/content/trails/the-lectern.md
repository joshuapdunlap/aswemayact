## Stop 1

Bush wanted people to build “useful trails through the enormous mass of the common record.” Public records are that mass. This trail goes through one small corner of it: a lectern bought on a state credit card in 2023, and the records that showed how its paperwork was handled.

## Stop 2

In 2023 the governor’s office bought a lectern from a Virginia event firm. With a 3% card fee the invoice came to $19,029. In October the Arkansas Advocate reported that emails released under the FOIA supported a whistleblower’s claim that a staff member had been told to write “to be reimbursed” on the original invoice and not to date the note. The governor’s office called the use of state funds an accounting error and denied wrongdoing.

Look at where the key email came from. It was written by a fiscal manager at the Department of Transformation and Shared Services, a state agency whose staff had traded emails with the governor’s office about the invoice. That agency sits outside the exemption the next stop describes.

## Stop 3

Arkansas exempts the unpublished memoranda, working papers and correspondence of the Governor, the Attorney General, legislators and appellate judges. The exemption attaches to the office holding the record. In the Advocate’s words, it “only applies to records that are in the possession of the exempted offices and individuals.” An email that a governor’s staffer sends to a non-exempt agency can be requested from that agency, unless some other exemption covers it. The [exemptions page](/manual/exemptions/) of the manual has the details.

## Stop 4

The legislature’s auditors looked at the purchase too. Arkansas Legislative Audit reviewed it from October 2023 to March 2024. The Advocate reported that the April 2024 report described “several instances of potential lawbreaking” by staff in how the lectern was bought, delivered and recorded. That is the auditors’ conclusion as the Advocate reported it, not mine. It is also another office’s record of the same events.

## Stop 5

The blogger who first reported the purchase had asked the governor’s office for, among other things, the lectern’s shipping records. The office denied two of his requests under the working-papers exemption, and he sued. On May 7, 2024, a Pulaski County circuit judge dismissed the case because the summons and complaint had not been served within 120 days of filing. He said he let the deadline pass on purpose so he could refile with more claims. Whatever his reason, the lesson for other requesters is the clock: a records suit can end on the service rule before a judge looks at a single record. The manual’s page on [what to do after a denial](/manual/denied/) covers that rule.

## Stop 6

If the record you want sits in an exempt office, ask who else handled it. A purchase leaves paper in the agency that processed the payment, and often in the vendor’s correspondence with that agency. Name the documents you want: the invoice, the purchase order, the card statement, the shipping records and the emails about them. Then [write the request](/manual/write/) to the agency the exemption doesn’t cover.
