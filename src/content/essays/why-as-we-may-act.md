In July 1945 *The Atlantic Monthly* printed an essay by Vannevar Bush called “As We May Think.”[^bush] Bush had spent the war directing American military research. Now he asked what scientists should do in peacetime, and his answer was about records. Knowledge was piling up faster than anyone could use it, and the ways we filed it no longer worked. He imagined a desk he called the memex, where one person could keep every book, letter and record, call any of them up quickly, and link any one to any other. “The process of tying two items together is the important thing,” he wrote.[^tying]

Near its end the essay turns to a warning. Humanity has built a great record, Bush says, but “he may perish in conflict before he learns to wield that record for his true good.”[^close] I named this site after that sentence. The title says *think*. The closing paragraph says *wield*, and this site starts from there. Bush doesn’t end on the threat, though. His last sentence calls this “a singularly unfortunate stage at which to terminate the process, or to lose hope as to the outcome.”[^last] I share that refusal, and I take it as a reason to act.

## Trails

Bush’s best idea was the trail. A memex user could link items into a named path, add notes along the way, and hand a copy to a friend. He expected a new kind of worker to come out of this: “a new profession of trail blazers, those who find delight in the task of establishing useful trails through the enormous mass of the common record.”[^blazers]

The memex was never built, but the trail outlived it. Douglas Engelbart quoted Bush at length in his 1962 report on augmenting human intellect and picked out the trail blazers by name.[^engelbart] Ted Nelson carried the idea onto the screen in 1972 with a paper called “As We Will Think.”[^nelson]

Government records are a common record in the plainest sense. Contracts, invoices, agendas, minutes and email are made with public money and kept by public offices, and in Arkansas any citizen may inspect and copy them.[^foia] Bush pictured trail blazing as a profession for specialists. I think of it as a civic skill that anyone can learn. A trail on this site is short. It moves through a few stops (a passage, a record, an idea, something a reporter or a court found) and ends with something you can do. Each stop is labeled with its kind and links to its source, so you can check my reading against the record.

## From thinking to acting

The turn from *think* to *act* isn’t mine. Paulo Freire defined praxis as “reflection and action upon the world in order to transform it.”[^freire] For him neither half was enough alone. Reflection without action was talk, and action without reflection was activism for its own sake. Later in the same book he wrote that “to speak a true word is to transform the world.”[^freire-word] Marx had put it more bluntly in 1845, in the last of his theses on Feuerbach: “The philosophers have only interpreted the world, in various ways; the point is to change it.”[^marx]

A records request is a small, practical case of praxis. You notice something in a budget line, a news story or a vote, and you form a question. Then you write a letter that names the record you want and the law that entitles you to it. When the record comes back, you know what the agency knew, and you can take that to the meeting where the decision gets made. You need no press pass and no lawyer for any of this. When an agency refuses, some requesters end up needing a lawyer anyway, and the manual says plainly where that road goes.

On paper, Arkansas makes the first steps easier than most states do. A public record is open on the spot unless the custodian certifies in writing that it is in active use or storage. Copies cost what copying costs, with no charge for staff time. Since August 2025 a public body with a website or a social media page has to post its agendas there before it meets.[^arkansas] What the state lacks is anyone standing between a requester and a courtroom. There is no ombudsman and no commission. Apart from a short route to an Attorney General’s opinion on personnel records, the only appeal from a denial is a lawsuit.[^appeal] That gap is why this site spends most of its pages on method.

## An inheritance, with its faults

I chose this lineage. It isn’t a straight line of progress from 1945 to now, and the people in it had faults that should be named.

Bush ran a large part of the war’s science. He proposed and chaired the National Defense Research Committee and directed the Office of Scientific Research and Development, whose uranium work fed into the Manhattan Project. He was at the Trinity test.[^nps] In the same month as the *Atlantic* essay he sent President Truman a plan for postwar science run largely by scientists. When Congress passed a bill on that model in 1947, Truman vetoed it. The bill, he wrote, would leave the agency “divorced from control by the people to an extent that it implies a distinct lack of faith in democratic processes.”[^truman]

The memex’s machinery came out of codebreaking. Michael Buckland traced the history of Bush’s microfilm selector and found that it “was never used operationally, except that it seems to have been used for cryptanalysis.”[^buckland] As far as anyone can tell, the machine behind the dream of a personal library did its only real work breaking codes. Wendy Hui Kyong Chun argues that treating the never-built memex as the origin of the web “should make us pause.”[^chun]

Bush’s imagined users are professional men. The lawyer, the patent attorney, the physician and the historian each get a turn at the desk.[^users] The women in the essay all sit at keyboards. A girl works the Voder at a World’s Fair, and a roomful of girls feeds a calculating machine with keypunches.[^keyboards] At a public meeting, at the stenotype, “a girl strokes its keys languidly and looks about the room and sometimes at the speaker with a disquieting gaze.”[^stenotype] Bush watches her and she watches the room, but she never speaks. Her job is to take down what other people say. The essay never pictures her using the record she makes, let alone wielding it.

These facts are part of the inheritance, and pointing Bush’s idea at public offices leaves them in place. What it changes is who holds the record and what it is for. Truman’s phrase, control by the people, is the better test, and it is the one this site tries to meet.

## What this site is, and what it isn’t

As We May Act teaches Arkansans how to get public records and what to do with them. The field manual explains the Freedom of Information Act in plain words and cites the statute behind each rule. The letter builder writes a request in your browser, and nothing you type is stored or sent. The source cards point to the places where records already live. The trails, which I sign, follow a question from a passage or a record to an action you can take.

The site teaches and links. It publishes no findings of its own about named officials. When a trail mentions a case, it links the reporting or the court record and says what was reported. It is not an ombudsman. It can’t tell you whether a denial was lawful, and it won’t file a request for you. Arkansas has no office that does either of those things.[^rcfp] What the site can do is help a requester get better at asking, and leave a trail for the next one.
