Three business days is the number most people remember. The statute starts somewhere else: records are due when you ask, and a short delay is allowed only on paper.

## Records: now, unless certified

The starting point is immediate access to any record that is not in active use or in storage.

<p class="law">§ 25-19-105(a)(6): “the obligation of a custodian to immediately provide to the requestor any responsive records not in active use or storage.”</p>
<!-- handbook p. 9 -->

If a record is "in active use or storage," the custodian must say so in writing and give you a date and an hour when you can have it. That date can be no more than three working days away.

<p class="law">§ 25-19-105(e): “the custodian shall certify this fact in writing to the applicant and set a date and hour within three (3) working days”</p>
<!-- handbook p. 15 -->

So the three working days are not a grace period every office gets. They are an exception with two conditions: the record is in use or in storage, and you get a written certification with a date and hour.

The handbook counts them as business days, so weekends do not count. Neither the statute nor the handbook says whether state holidays do; if one falls in the window, the cautious reading adds a day.

<p class="law">AG Handbook, Questions &amp; Answers, asked whether the three days are business or calendar days: “Three business days.”</p>
<!-- handbook p. 43 -->

An office also may not make three days its habit. The Attorney General has said a standing practice of waiting three days to answer has no footing in the act, and that an office that can tell at once that a request is too vague, or will be denied, should say so promptly.

<p class="law">Op. Att’y Gen. 2006-093: “there is no basis in the FOIA for any standard policy of waiting for three days to respond to FOIA requests.”</p>
<!-- not in the handbook: Op. Att’y Gen. 2006-093, pp. 6–7 -->

Requests for personnel and evaluation records run on their own short clock: the custodian has twenty-four hours to decide whether they are exempt and to try to tell you and the employee. What happens next is on [Exemptions that matter](/manual/exemptions/).

<p class="law">§ 25-19-105(c)(3)(A): “determine within twenty-four (24) hours of the receipt of the request whether the records are exempt from disclosure”</p>
<!-- handbook p. 14 -->

## Keeping time on a request

<ol class="step-list" role="list">
<li>

Write down the day and time you asked, and keep a copy of a written request.

</li>
<li>

If the records are not handed over or sent, ask whether they are in active use or storage. If they are, ask for the written certification with its date and hour.

</li>
<li>

If you got neither the records nor a written certificate, they are already late. At the latest, a certificate's date can be the third working day after your request; count a day more if a state holiday falls in between.

</li>
<li>

If the third working day passes with no records and no certification, or the certification's date passes with no records, go to [Denied, now what](/manual/denied/).

</li>
</ol>

## Meetings: notice and agenda

The meetings half of the act has its own deadlines. Anyone may ask to be told the time and place of a body's regular meetings.

<p class="law">§ 25-19-106(b)(1)(A): “The time and place of each regular public meeting shall be furnished to anyone who requests the information.”</p>
<!-- handbook p. 17 -->

That notice must come at least three days ahead, unless another law sets a different timeline.

<p class="law">§ 25-19-106(b)(1)(B): “at least three (3) days before the public meeting takes place”</p>
<!-- handbook p. 17 -->

For an emergency or special meeting, the person calling it must notify the newspapers, radio and television stations in the county, and other news outlets that cover the body and have asked to be told, at least two hours ahead.

<p class="law">§ 25-19-106(b)(2)(B): “at least two (2) hours before the public meeting takes place”</p>
<!-- handbook p. 17 -->

[Act 505 of 2025](https://arkleg.state.ar.us/Acts/FTPDocument?path=%2FACTS%2F2025R%2FPublic%2F&file=505.pdf&ddBienniumSession=2025%2F2025R) (SB 227, approved April 10, 2025) added an online duty. If the body, or the public entity it governs, has a website or a social media page, it must post the meeting's time, place and date there, and post its most current agenda at least three days before a regular meeting and at least two hours before an emergency or special one.

<p class="law">§ 25-19-106(b)(3)(A): “The time, place, and date of a public meeting shall be published online if the governing body … maintains a website or social media page”</p>
<!-- handbook p. 17 -->

<p class="law">§ 25-19-106(b)(3)(B)(i): the most current agenda goes online “at least three (3) days before a regular public meeting and at least two (2) hours before an emergency or special public meeting.”</p>
<!-- handbook p. 17 -->

A posted agenda can still grow: items may be added after it goes up.

<p class="law">§ 25-19-106(b)(3)(B)(ii): “does not preclude a governing body from adding items to an agenda after the agenda has been furnished or posted”</p>
<!-- handbook p. 17 -->

## Bond votes: thirty days

[Act 992 of 2025](https://arkleg.state.ar.us/Acts/FTPDocument?path=%2FACTS%2F2025R%2FPublic%2F&file=992.pdf&ddBienniumSession=2025%2F2025R) (SB 614, approved April 22, 2025) added subsection (j) to the meetings section. It applies when a governing body breaks the open-meetings rules in the course of authorizing bonds or other debt. A court can undo the vote only within thirty days of the day it happened.

<p class="law">§ 25-19-106(j)(1): “may only invalidate the action by the governing body authorizing the issuance of bonds within thirty (30) days of the date the action occurred.”</p>
<!-- handbook p. 21 -->

When a court finds the violation later than that, subsection (j)(2) gives two outcomes joined by "or." The body cures the violation within thirty days by giving notice, disclosing the violation at a public meeting and voting again in the open. Otherwise the court imposes a $1,000 civil penalty on each member it finds responsible, paid to the person who brought the claim within thirty days and not from public money.

<p class="law">§ 25-19-106(j)(2)(B)(i): “a civil penalty of one thousand dollars ($1,000) on each individual member of the governing body”</p>
<!-- handbook p. 21 -->

<p class="law">§ 25-19-106(j)(2)(B)(ii)(b): the penalty shall “Not be satisfied by public funds.”</p>
<!-- handbook p. 21 -->
